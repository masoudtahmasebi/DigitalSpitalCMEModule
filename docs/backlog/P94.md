## P94-01 — the percentage was a fraction of the requirement, not of the video

### What was actually happening

The union **was** being checked. Measured on the build the client was looking
at, a fresh ten-second video reads:

| watched            | percent | gaps |
| ------------------ | ------- | ---- |
| 1 s                | 14 %    | 1–7  |
| 3 s                | 42 %    | 3–7  |
| a scrub to the end | 0 %     | 0–7  |
| 0–7                | 100 %   | —    |

So one second did not make it complete, and dragging the scrub bar still earned
nothing. What the client saw was the last second of a video they had already
covered to 0–6 — the union closing its final gap, which is the rule working.

**And the complaint is still right**, for a reason the arithmetic hides. P93-01
put the tail grace in the _denominator_, so the number was a fraction of the
**requirement** — seven seconds — while its label said _angesehen_ and the
learner could see ten. A number whose referent is invisible cannot be checked by
the person reading it, and the only available inference is the one that was
drawn: that it is not counting properly.

### The rule now

The percentage is a fraction of the **video**, always. The grace lives in one
branch instead:

> when there is nothing left for the learner to go and watch, the whole video is
> credited — including its last three seconds.

Same table, after:

| watched            | percent | gaps |
| ------------------ | ------- | ---- |
| 1 s                | 10 %    | 1–7  |
| 3 s                | 30 %    | 3–7  |
| a scrub to the end | 10 %    | 0–7  |
| 0–6                | 60 %    | 6–7  |
| 0–7                | 100 %   | —    |

Every figure is now something the client can check against the clock under the
player. The scrub earns its one real second and nothing more.

### The property that falls out, which is the point

```
watchedPercent(…) === 100   ⟺   uncoveredSpans(…) is empty
```

Two screens draw those — the percentage beside the section title and _"Diese
Stellen fehlen noch"_ under the player — and P68-02 was precisely the state
where one said finished and the other did not. The biconditional makes that
disagreement unrepresentable, and it is asserted over nine cases rather than
asserted once.

### The tolerance had to become a budget

`uncoveredSpans` forgives a gap below one playback sample, which was safe while
it only _listed_ things. Now that it decides completion, a per-gap tolerance
would be a way to skip content: a client reporting `[0,0.24] [0.49,0.73] …`
clears every gap individually and leaves half the video unwatched. It is summed
first, so at most a quarter second of the **whole file** can ever be forgiven,
whatever shape the holes are. There is a test that builds exactly that attack.

The list is also never emptied by its own display filter — a learner told
nothing is missing while the gate withholds completion is P85-01's report, and
is the one state this must not produce.

### Migrated, not deleted

The `+3` fixture durations from P93-01 (`903`, `103`, `203`) went back to the
honest `900`, `100`, `200`: the arithmetic no longer needs the denominator to be
round, because the denominator is the video. One old case changed meaning rather
than numbers — "never closes a hole in the middle, however small" now has a
one-second hole, and the tenth-of-a-second case beside it asserts the agreement
with the gaps list that P85-01 asked for.

## P94-02 — the colours, and the call to action that was not one

### The palette

`Player-Ansicht-*` draws a finished module as an **orange disc with a white
tick** and a module under way as an orange disc with pause bars. Ours were green
and teal — a generic status palette rather than this product's.

Orange is not decoration: `tailwind.preset.js` already says what the token means
— _"orange is resume the thing you started"_ — which is what a module in a list
you are working down is. It is also a **branded** variable, so a customer
restyling the widget restyles these; `status.completed` was a fixed green no
customer could reach.

### One distinction worth keeping

The layout gives the module you are inside a **pause** glyph and the chapter you
are on a **play** arrow. That is not a stylistic wobble: the module is a
container you are part-way through, the chapter is the thing in front of you.
`state.inProgress` — _"Wird bearbeitet"_ — is the module's; _"Wird angesehen"_
stays the chapter's. Announcing "Wird angesehen" on a module would be a claim
about five chapters at once.

### The sidebar is one panel, not five cards

Hairlines between rows, as drawn. Five bordered cards read as five separate
things rather than one outline of one course.

### The call to action

Two halves, and the second is the one that cost the client a wrong turn.

- **It was teal.** The reasoning on record was that the accent marks the course
  in progress and the exam is the way out of it. The client looked at a finished
  video and found no call to action, which settles it: the one thing a physician
  should do next is the one thing in the accent colour, whatever that thing is.
- **There were two controls for it.** `nextAvailableContent` returns whatever the
  server has open, and once a module's video is done that is the module's own
  Lernerfolgskontrolle — so the row under the video drew a second control for
  it, labelled with the exam's own title and nothing to say it _was_ an exam.
  That is the _"when i click on the title of lernerfolgskontrolle it goes to
  lernerfolgskontrolle, but the user does not know it"_ half, and it is §9.4.
  One exam, one control, and it is the orange one.

### The save icon

The layout puts a floppy before _"Ihr Fortschritt wird automatisch
gespeichert"_. Decorative — the sentence says the same thing in words — but it
is what makes a physician notice the reassurance at all.

Each of the three behaviours was watched to go red: the teal variant, the
duplicate control, and the module glyph.

## P94-03 — a physician arriving from WordPress becomes a participant

**The client's decision, which unblocked this:**

> _"basically when the info for a user comes to our system from a wordpress
> system, if there is no detail for that user based on their email, a new
> participant would be created for them, if there is a participant already in
> the system, they can go on."_

### What was missing, and where

P1-02 provisioned the **person** from the token — `provision_learner` writes
`users` and `user_identities` — and stopped. Nothing anywhere wrote the
`user_customers` membership or the `user_roles` grant that
`resolveTenantContext` then requires. So every physician reaching the widget
from MEDICE's site met a 403 naming a user id they had never seen.

That is §9.3 with the missing half one layer _up_: the rule was enforced, the
thing it enforced against was never created.

### The rule now

In the guard, after the person is resolved and before the tenant context is
built: a **`keycloak`** learner holding **no grants at all** is made a `learner`
of the customer the project binding names, and audited as
`auth.learner_provisioned`.

Both halves of the client's sentence hold: an unseen physician becomes a
participant, a known one goes on. The two statements are
`ParticipantRepository.createMembership`'s, deliberately — a portal participant
and a WordPress participant must be the same kind of row or the console lists
one and not the other.

### Three conditions, and why each is there

| Condition                  | Why                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `provider === "keycloak"`  | The token is the customer's own IdP vouching for this person, which is the trust ADR-0003 is built on. A `local` project's participants come from the portal's invite flow; there an unknown subject really is somebody who should not be let in. |
| `grants.length === 0`      | A person who already learns with customer A and opens customer B's project is not an unprovisioned arrival. Granting them B would be privilege escalation dressed as convenience — and invisible, because the request would simply start working. |
| the **binding's** customer | Not anything the learner's token asserts. The binding is the platform's own row, written by an operator.                                                                                                                                          |

### The half deliberately not built

**Matching a WordPress arrival onto an existing account by email.**

Within one realm it decides nothing that the subject has not already decided.
Across realms it decides something dangerous, and `user.repository.ts` has said
so since P21-01: a provider that does not verify email addresses could assert
its way into a physician's CME history and EFN, and the platform cannot tell
which providers verify. Joining two credentials to one person is P21-05 —
deliberate, audited, and not something an authentication path infers.

So if the same physician has both a portal password and a WordPress login, they
are two records until somebody merges them on purpose. Worth a decision; not
worth making silently.

### `runInTenant`, on a repository whose header says it does not

`users`, `user_identities` and `user_roles` are not tenant-scoped — role
resolution is what the tenant context is built _from_. `user_customers` **is**,
under FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY, and an insert on the bare pool matches no policy
and writes nothing while reporting success. That is §9.6 exactly, and it is why
the membership goes through `runInTenant` and the grant does not have to.

### What proves it

- `auth-catalog-pipeline.integration.test.ts` — the unseen learner reaches 200
  and holds exactly one grant for exactly one customer, with the membership
  beside it; and the control, that a learner granted at customer A is still
  refused at customer B and that the refused request granted nothing.
- `wordpress.spec.ts` — in a browser, through the plugin, over a real Keycloak
  token: a physician nobody has provisioned sees the catalogue. That test
  asserted the **opposite** yesterday and said so in its own comment: _"the day
  a provisioning path exists, it fails here — loudly, in the file whose subject
  is the WordPress channel."_ It did.
- Neutering the call turns the first red; removing the `grants.length` condition
  turns the second red **and** an existing tenant-isolation test.

`grantLearnerRole` is deleted from the harness. It stood in for the missing path
and said so; a stand-in that outlives the thing it stood in for is the next
person's wrong turn.

## P94-04 — allowed origins are patterns, on the project

> _"the ALLOWED_ORIGINS should be configurable from the `Projekte` entity and it
> should be possible to add multiple entries, for dev staging and prod urls with
>
> - and possible to add all of the domain, even localhost with a port."_

### Half of this already existed, and saying so matters

Since P18-04 the origins live on `projects.embed_origins`, are editable in the
console under Organisation → Projekte, and `ALLOWED_ORIGINS` carries only this
installation's own two browser origins — so adding a customer has not widened
another customer's permission for some time.

What did not exist is the pattern part, and it is the part that decides whether
the setting is usable. One customer is several origins: `www`, a staging host, a
preview deployment per branch, a developer's machine on whatever port Vite
picked this morning. Enumerating them means an operator editing a platform
setting every time somebody spins one up — so the setting gets left wide, or the
embed silently fails, and a CORS failure is invisible from the server (§9.13).

### The grammar

| Pattern                   | Matches                                         |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| `https://www.example.com` | exactly that origin                             |
| `https://*.example.com`   | any sub-domain, at any depth — **not** the apex |
| `http://localhost:*`      | that host on any port                           |
| `https://*.example.com:*` | both at once                                    |

The apex exclusion is the surprising one, so the field's own hint says it: a
customer serving from both `example.com` and `www.example.com` writes two lines.

### What is refused, and why it is the whole point

A bare `*`, `*://…`, `https://*`, `https://*.de`. This API answers with
`Access-Control-Allow-Credentials: true`, and the fetch specification forbids
that together with a wildcard origin **precisely** because it would let any page
on the web make authenticated requests as a signed-in physician. A wildcard is
therefore always anchored to a name somebody has registered — at least two
labels after the star.

A star anywhere but as the whole leftmost label is refused too: `https://ww*.x.de`
reads as if it means something and would be a different rule from the one the
operator thinks they wrote.

And the matching is component-wise, never a suffix comparison. `evil-medice.de`
is a name anybody can register, one character from the customer's, and a
`endsWith("medice.de")` would hand it a physician's session.

### Four layers, and the seam between them is tested

| Layer                             | What it does                                                                        |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `@ds/domain/embed-origin.ts`      | the grammar and the matcher, exhaustively unit-tested                               |
| `authoring.dto.ts`                | refuses a bad entry at the API boundary                                             |
| `is_origin_list` (migration 0045) | the same grammar as a CHECK, because a console is not the only way rows get written |
| `EmbedOriginRegistry`             | matches with the **same** function the console validates with                       |

The last one matters most: two implementations of "does this origin match" would
be the same defect as two implementations of a percentage, on the side where
disagreeing means letting a page in. And because the SQL is a regex and the
TypeScript is a parser — deliberately, a CHECK cannot call the application —
`embed-origins.integration.test.ts` compares the two over thirteen cases in both
directions. A column that accepts what the rule refuses leaves an unusable row;
a column that refuses what the rule accepts is a form saying "Gespeichert" over a
save that failed.

### The form now names the line, not the field

`invalidEmbedOriginPatterns` existed for this and had no caller, which is P41-01's
shape exactly. The API's 422 names `embedOrigins`, so an operator with six
origins and one typo is told the field and left to find it. The values are shown
rather than hidden — §9.5's rule is about values that identify a person, and a
hostname somebody typed thirty seconds ago is not one.

### Also found by the gates

`security/detect-unsafe-regex` refused the natural DNS-label regex, which is the
same refusal `isOrigin` hit twice before P18-04 replaced it with `URL`. The rule
is conservative about backtracking and it is also right: a grammar this small
does not need a backtracking engine, so the label check is a loop.
